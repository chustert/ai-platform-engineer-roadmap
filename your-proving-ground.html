Your proving ground: a real product

Most learning roadmaps have you build everything as a portfolio system with no real users. That is the weakest kind of evidence for both target roles: one asks whether you have "shipped a product that other people use", the other whether you have "actually operated non-deterministic LLM workloads in production". The fix is to anchor the roadmap in a real product.

Pick one AI-powered product with real users and do the model-economics, retrieval, observability, evaluation, reliability and security work of Phases 2–10 on it. Traces from real requests, real cost per task, retrieval failures on real content and a regression suite built from real complaints are far more convincing than the same artifacts built against synthetic data – and the improvements pay for themselves.

Where it can come from, in order of strength: a product you already own or run; your employer's product, where you have permission to work on its AI features and talk about the results; an open-source project with active users; or, failing all of those, a small product you ship in the first two months – an AI search-and-answer feature for a community or business website, an internal assistant for a local organisation, a niche tool you would use yourself. Retrieval-backed "answer questions over this content" products are ideal because they exercise almost every phase. The rest of this document calls it "your product".

Forge, the capstone, remains the place for internal developer-platform work: coding-agent enablement, PR review, context engines. Make it something you actually use every day on your product and other work, so that it too has a real user. If a team you work with is open to it, piloting parts of it there (a shared agent setup, a PR-review agent, an MCP server) is the most direct rehearsal available for the "drive adoption through pairing, not mandates" part of the Staff role.

Keep the product in view

Run a continuous product track alongside the phases: talk to your product's users, decide what not to build, and judge each technical improvement by whether it made a customer's job easier. Both postings treat that judgment as the scarce skill.
